                \subsubsection{Kinematic Baselines}
                    \paragraph{Hyperparameters}
                            The \ac{CV} model learns to only use the most recent timestep's velocity. This is an assumption that is also applied in most \ac{CV} definitions, which I relaxed for these experiments. With increasing model complexity, the best context size increases. The Hybrid Model learns to switch to \ac{CV} at a relatively small $\tau_\text{rot}$  of 29.28 °/min. This indicates, that straight line motion more prevelant, than motion with a single arc in our dataset.
            
                    \paragraph{Performance}
                        Model Performance suggests, that to outperform straight line motion prediction, simple arc estimations are not sufficient.
                        
              Figure~\ref{fig:adepredhorizon} shows the ADE per prediction step
                      for all models. While \ac{CV} grows approximately linearly, CTRV and
                      CTRV Arc diverge at longer horizons. One cause for this difference is the fact, that the distance of the circular arc generated by CTRV grows superlinearly with the number of prediction whereas a wrong velocity estimate in \ac{CV}
                        produces a constant lateral offset that accumulates linearly. This can be easily grasped by looking at the upper rows of Figures \ref{fig:domain1} and \ref{fig:domain3}. %reflects the fact, that wrong rotations result in a circular arc and can get penalised ~quadratically when calculating the distance, while straight deviations get a linear penalty.
                        
                \subsubsection{Stochastic Filters}
                    \paragraph{Hyperparamters}
    
                    
                    In the \ac{KF}, position changes are a linear function of the velocity
                    state, so positional errors remain small as long as the velocity estimate
                    is reasonable.  A small $q_\text{pos}$ is sufficient.
                    In the \ac{EKF}, position depends nonlinearly on the turn rate $\omega_k$
                    through trigonometric terms, meaning that even a small error in the
                    estimated rotation propagates into a larger positional error.
                    The filter compensates by assigning a larger $q_\text{pos}$, it learned, that position predictions are less reliable when
                    rotation is involved. The near-zero $q_\text{rot}$, combined with the initialisation
                    $\omega_0 = 0$ and a minimal context length $n_\text{ctx} = 2$,
                    indicates that the \ac{EKF} learns to maintain an approximately
                    zero turn rate throughout, and reduces the underlying \ac{CTRV} to
                    straight-line motion with negligible curvature.
                
                    \paragraph{Performance}
                    The \ac{KF} does not outperform the \ac{CV} model. This means, the bottleneck of the Filter is not in the state estimation, but rather the underlying model assumption.
                    Also, since the \ac{CV} learned to only look at the last step, the Markov assumption of the \ac{KF} does not come with any drawbacks.
                    
                    Despite only allowing minimal curvature, the \ac{EKF} outperforms the \ac{KF} significantly (\ref{fig:cd_test}), most pronounced in RMSE, which makes sense, since errors in curvature lead to outliers, which are more heavily penalised by the \ac{RMSE} Term.\\
                    The  violin plots in Figure \ref{fig:violince} confirm that the \ac{EKF} distribution is shifted towards slightly lower errors compared to \ac{CV} and TiREX, but exhibits a similar spread and long upper tails, indicating that outlier handling is not improved. 
                   
                
                \subsubsection{Foundation Models}
                %\paragraph{Hyperparameters}
                    TiREX runs best with a context length of 1, which aligns with the constant-velocity 
                assumption and explains the % near-identical performance between TiREX and CV across \me{statistically indifferent?}
                statistical indifference between TiREX and \ac{CV} across all point-forecast metrics. Not only the performance, but also the generated trajectories are similar to those of the \ac{CV} models (Figures \ref{fig:domain1} to \ref{fig:domain4}), indicating the foundation model predicts straight line kinematics. A distributional comparison of per‑track \ac{RMSE} (Figure \ref{fig:violince}) reveals that \ac{CV} and TiREX have nearly identical violin shapes with similar medians, interquartile ranges, and tail behaviour. They confirm, that TiREX effectively learns \ac{CV} kinematics without any domain‑specific training.

                    %\paragraph{Performance}
                    If we consider Table \ref{tab:results_exp0_foundation_prob}, we notice, that TiREX produces extremely narrow prediction intervals compared to Chronos-2. Narrow intervals can indicate either high accuracy or 
                    poor calibration. Inspecting the coverage reveals the latter: only $5.9\%$ of true 
                    future positions fall within the nominal $80\%$ prediction intervals of TiREX, far below 
                    the ideal coverage of $0.80$. Chronos-2, by contrast, achieves a near ideal coverage of $0.769$. \\                    
                    The Winkler score of TiREX is lower than that of Chronos-2. The Winkler score rewards narrow 
                    intervals when they happen to have the true value, and penalises misses in proportion 
                    to interval width. Since the intervals of TiREX are so narrow, each miss only causes a small absolute penalty. \\                    
                    It follows, that if we want to use a foundation model and also have an accurate quantification of uncertainty, TiREX is unsuitable, despite better performance in ADE and FDE.
                

 

                \subsubsection{LSTM}
                
                    \paragraph{Hyperparameters}
                    The selected hyperparameter configuration is illustrated in Table \ref{tab:hpo_results_exp0} and Figure \ref{fig:paralelcord}. Figure \ref{fig:succhalfing} shows the successive halving process during optimisation. As expected, several poor configurations are discarded early, while the better trials survive longer and are refined further by early stopping. A few weak trials still run comparatively long because the initial 16 \ac{HPO} evaluations were not pruned, which was intentional.
                    
                    Figure \ref{fig:paralelcord} presents the parallel coordinate plot of the optimisation results. Optuna identifies the input feature set as the most important hyperparameter%das geht nicht aus dem graphen hervor, sondern aus einem anderen. ist das hier ok?
                    , and the plot shows a relatively clear band around the optimum configuration.
                    
                    Interestingly, the best configuration does not use the full feature set but excludes \texttt{rot}. While one might expect that adding more input features should always improve performance, as we can confirm for all other values by looking at the gradient, this result indicates that \ac{ROT} does not contribute useful information in this setting. This is consistent with the \ac{EKF} results and suggests that the \texttt{\ac{ROT}} feature we derived is either noisy or only weakly informative for the motion patterns considered here. Future work should revisit our choise to derive this feature ourselfes and consider using the \textit{heading} field instead. %careful here. this conclusion is correct, but ekf itself does not safe that, since there, rot was implicit
                    
                    \paragraph{Performance}
                    The \acp{LSTM} show the strongest forecasting performance among all evaluated models by a large margin (Table \ref{tab:results_exp0} and Figure \ref{fig:adepredhorizon}). Compared to the baselines, they reduce the prediction error substantially. The longer the prediction horizon, the bigger is the outperformance to the baselines. By looking at figures \ref{fig:domain1} to \ref{fig:domain4}, we can see one cause of this: The \acp{LSTM} particularly excel at estimating the correct velocity. This makes the biggest difference in the lock domain, as most trajectories include accelerations to either start or stop vessel movement.\\ Overall, the network appears to learn motion patterns that generalise better than the handcrafted assumptions encoded in \ac{CV}, CTRV, and the filters.\\
                    The \ac{RMSE} violin plots in Figure \ref{fig:violince} further show that the error distribution of the \ac{LSTM} is shifted toward lower values than the other models. This indicates that the model performs better on most trajectories, while a smaller number of difficult cases still produce comparatively high errors. Compared with the foundation models, the LSTM therefore achieves not only better average accuracy, but also a more favorable distribution of prediction errors.\\                    
                    A deeper comparison between the plain \ac{LSTM} and the domain-aware variant is presented in Exp \Romannum{3} (\ref{sec:exp3}).

                \subsubsection{Overall}
                    The monthly trends in Figure \ref{fig:monthlytrends-all} are consistent across all models, which suggests that they are not caused by the underlying motion models, but rather reflect properties of the dataset itself.
                   Without data from multiple years, we cannot determine whether this
                    constitutes a seasonal trend.
                    Possible causes could be, but are not limited to: Large Vessels being easier to predict and being more represented in Summer, or  different traffic characteristics, and as a result more evasive manoeuvres in winter. The simplest explanation is higher data availability in the
                    summer months combined with hyperparameter optimisation and model training being fitted to those months.
                    However, this does not explain why the same trend is visible for Chronos-2, which was neither trained nor tuned on this data.
                    We therefore take this simply as a confirmation that using a full year of data was the right decision, and note that the evaluation timeframe has a significant impact on reported results. Future work should investigate this further by incorporating older data. That comes with no additional coding effort, but was not done here to keep training and evaluation times reasonable.
                    %The pronounced monthly variations in RMSE are highly consistent across all evaluated models, which suggests that these trends are driven primarily by properties of the dataset rather than by specific modelling choices. However, since the evaluation is restricted to a single year and the available metadata does not allow us to disentangle traffic volume, operational patterns, and environmental factors, we cannot conclusively attribute these effects to genuine seasonality. Instead, they should be interpreted as an indication that data availability and traffic composition over time have a non-negligible impact on the reported error metrics.
                    
                    Beyond the metric tables, Figure~\ref{fig:cd_test} provides an overall comparison of the evaluated model families. The critical difference diagram shows a clear ranking structure: the two LSTM-based models occupy the top positions, followed by the EKF and Chronos-2, while remaining baselines form a middle group with mostly non-significant pairwise differences. The CTRV-based models rank last.\\                    
                    From this perspective, learned motion models are clearly best suited to capture the non-trivial vessel dynamics in the evaluated AIS data. At the same time, the results show that stochastic filters remain competitive and can outperform foundation models when the underlying motion assumptions match the task reasonably well. In contrast, increasing kinematic complexity alone does not necessarily improve performance, as the CTRV variants consistently remain among the weakest approaches.

\subsection{Discussion}
\paragraph{Domain comparison}
The evaluation across domains shows, that model performance can change drastically between them. Domains are not equivalent in terms of \enquote{difficulty} and all represent distinct, motion patterns and uncertainty characteristics. 
A notable observation is that the optimised hyperparameters vary substantially across domains. This applies not only to the learned models, but also to the classical filters, where the optimised values for measurement noise, process noise, and initial covariance differ considerably between domains.  In particular, the lock class shows very distinct hyperparameters. The lock domain is the only domain that was augmented. Its distinct hyperparameter configuration is consistent with the expectation that augmentation alters the effective training distribution.  Future work should investigate whether sampling and augmentation should be applied more uniformly across all classes to reduce such asymmetries. 
The Channel domain achieves the lowest errors for several models, especially the \ac{LSTM}, which suggests more regular vessel movements or a more homogeneous data distribution. By contrast, the Harbour domain remains comparatively difficult to predict, which may reflect a more diverse set of manoeuvres, denser traffic, or a broader range of trajectory patterns, consistent with the open space available in harbour areas, vs the other classes (Figure \ref{fig:osm_assigned_kiel}). The lock and channel domain show the biggest relative  improvement when moving from kinematic to learned models. The most likely reasons for that would be curvature combinations in channels the \ac{CTRV} and \ac{CV} model cannot handle, as well as accelerations in locks that the motion models cannot capture.
 \paragraph{Comparison to mixed training}  
         The benefit of per-domain training over mixed training, when evaluating on the respective domain, increases with model complexity: simple kinematic baselines show almost no change, whereas EKF and \ac{LSTM} exhibits large performance drops under mixed training.  Only for the harbour domain, this difference was not statistically significant for the \ac{LSTM} ({fig:cd-diagrams-a}), most likely because of the similarity of both datasets.  We can conclude, that high-capacity models are most sensitive to domain heterogeneity and either require explicit domain conditioning or separate models per domain to fully realise their potential. 

        The experiment also shows, that the benefit of per-domain training is not uniform across all domains. For all models, Harbour and River show only minor differences between mixed and domain-specific training, the Channel and Lock domains benefit more. This indicates that Channel and Lock represent the most distinct motion regimes while the separation of Harbour and River is less critical for model performance in this setup. This should be considered when extending the model to a new set of domains.

        \subsection{Discussion}
   
         Interestingly, the LSTM + Domain* model, which reuses the hyperparameters of the baseline LSTM, outperforms the LSTM + Domain model that was tuned in a separate Optuna study with statistical significance (\ref{fig:cd_exp3}). During hyperparameter search for LSTM + Domain, no configuration better than the initial 16 trials was found before early stopping terminated the study. This suggests that the search may have stopped prematurely, and that the domain-aware model could benefit from a more exhaustive tuning procedure. Future work could revisit this experiment with increased patience or a larger budget for hyperparameter optimisation to fully exploit the potential of the domain-aware architecture.
        
        The consistent outperformance of the LSTM + Domain models  against the standard \acp{LSTM} in Table \ref{tab:results_exp3} and Figure \ref{fig:violindomaine} indicate a clear and robust dominance of the domain-aware model over the baseline.
        These results provide strong evidence that explicitly encoding domain information into the model architecture consistently improves trajectory prediction performance in all considered domains. 
        The domain-aware model exhibits a lower median error and lower first and third quartiles, indicating consistently better typical performance. The interquartile ranges of both models are comparable, but the baseline LSTM shows more extreme outliers. This suggests, that it is more prone to occasional large errors. While the best-case \acs{RMSE} of the baseline is slightly lower in this particular run, this effect was not consistent across the additional ten training runs and does not affect the overall dominance of the domain-aware model.
